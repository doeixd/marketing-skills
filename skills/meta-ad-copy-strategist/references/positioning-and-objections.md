# Positioning, feature-to-benefit ladders, purchase objections and drawbacks

Use this reference after the evidence ledger and before ad writing. The goal is **a differentiated, defensible hypothesis about a purchase decision**. Positioning is not merely a catchy line. For the full five-part alternatives-first positioning workflow, also read [market positioning](market-positioning.md); for price/value, read [product value and pricing](product-value-and-pricing.md).

## 1. Features → consequences → buyer benefits → desired outcomes

Start with confirmed product properties. Apply repeated **"so what?"** questions until there is a benefit someone in a particular situation would value.

| Layer | Question | Example (hypothetical vintage-style ring) |
|---|---|---|
| Attribute | What is objectively true? | Intricate, vintage-inspired detailing, if confirmed by product/photo |
| Practical/aesthetic effect | What does it do or look like? | Visually more elaborate than a plain band |
| Benefit | Why might someone want that? | They can choose a more expressive accessory |
| Outcome / identity | What progress matters? | They want something that reflects their taste |
| Claim support | What validates the chain? | Product photos support style; reviews needed for actual motive |

Distinguish **product truth** from **subjective benefit**. An appearance may be visible; claiming that it makes buyers feel confident or is "widely loved" needs evidence. A buyer may value a simple look rather than elaborate detailing — benefits are audience-dependent.

## 2. Motivation taxonomy (not a rigid checklist)

- **Functional**: performance, compatibility, comfort, durability, convenience, ease of use.
- **Economic**: value, affordability, total cost, predictable expenses; discounts only when active and verifiable.
- **Aesthetic / sensory**: look, texture, color, fit with the customer's style.
- **Emotional**: sentimental connection, joy, delight, nostalgia, reassurance.
- **Social / expressive**: signaling taste, identity, gift thoughtfulness, professional presentation.
- **Occasion / timing**: holiday, birthday, milestone, seasonal need, replacement event.
- **Discovery / novelty**: unusual design, exploration, collectibility.
- **Brand-specific**: trust, demonstrated expertise, transparent policies, service; only use verified claims.

Many products involve multiple motives. Generate separate concepts when motives conflict; do not cram them into one paragraph.

## 3. Awareness and message selection

Adapt Schwartz's awareness-stage framing as a **creative lens**, not a fact about users or a way to infer sensitive traits:

| Stage | What they likely know | Good message purpose | Avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unaware | No active interest yet | Show a relevant appealing use case or situation | Inventing a painful problem |
| Problem / occasion aware | Has need or upcoming decision | Name the goal and demonstrate relevance | Overexplaining the category |
| Solution / category aware | Comparing kinds of solutions | Explain advantages/tradeoffs of the product type | Unsubstantiated superiority |
| Product aware | Knows the product or brand | Address an objection; show proof or real distinction | Repeating broad lifestyle slogans |
| Most aware | Has considered buying | Clarify actual offer, price, availability, delivery terms | False scarcity or pressure |

If the audience is broad, vary creative angles rather than assigning demographic stereotypes. Retargeting may shift copy emphasis toward unanswered questions; avoid disclosing or assuming individual browsing behavior in ad language.

## 4. Objection vs. drawback vs. friction

- **Objection**: a buyer's expressed doubt ("Will the size fit?").
- **Drawback**: a real limitation or tradeoff (e.g., no half sizes, if verified).
- **Friction**: difficulty making the purchase (poor sizing chart, unclear return terms, complicated checkout).
- **Alternative advantage**: something a competitor or nonpurchase may do better.

The AI must not erase or rhetorically bypass real weaknesses. It should classify each concern into one of four responses:

1. **Answer with evidence**: real specifications, comparison, demo, reviews, warranty, shipping SLA, terms.
2. **Reduce uncertainty with experience/design**: sizing photos, video, FAQ, delivery estimator, more informative landing page.
3. **Acknowledge a tradeoff**: for buyers who prioritize another attribute, a competing solution may be more suitable.
4. **Escalate a product/process issue**: quality control, returns, support or fulfillment need fixing; don't paper over them with copy.

### Objection-response matrix

| Concern | Buyer question | Acceptable evidence / action | Never assert without proof |
|---|---|---|---|
| Quality | Will it look/feel as expected? | Real close-ups, material specs, verified reviews | "Premium", "best", "lifetime" |
| Fit | What if size is wrong? | Size guide, dimensions, actual exchange policy | "Fits everyone" |
| Value | Why is it worth the price? | Specific differences, included components, credible provenance | Fictitious savings or retail comparison |
| Delivery | Will it arrive when I need it? | Actual estimated transit and processing time | Guaranteed dates without backing |
| Trust | Is this a real business? | Accessible contact, verifiable history, policies | Unsupported social proof |
| Returns | Can I change my mind? | Exact return window and exclusions | "Risk-free" without qualification |
| Durability | Will it last? | Testing, material properties, care guidance | Unverified wear or lifespan promises |
| Relevance | Why buy rather than do nothing? | Clear use case, real occasion, product utility | Made-up emergency |

An objection may be addressed in **visual creative or on the destination**, not necessarily the 125-character opening.

## 5. Differentiation and competitor alternatives

Assess: (1) unique attributes, (2) rare attribute combinations, (3) credible advantages on common criteria, (4) emotional/style territory, (5) brand trust and service, (6) real offer. Check *substitutes*, not only direct competitors: cheap costume jewelry, another category of gift, a gift card, existing jewelry, or buying nothing.

Use fair comparisons. State the dimension, exact evidence and limitations. Do not imply "only brand" or competitor deficiencies without substantiation.

## 6. Selecting angles to test

For every candidate angle capture:

```text
angle_id:
buyer_situation:
job_to_be_done:
core_motivation:
feature_or_offer:
benefit_and_outcome:
main_objection_or_tradeoff:
evidence_ids:
confidence: high | medium | low
creative_visual_needed:
landing_page_proof:
hypothesis: For [situation], [specific message] will [metric] vs [comparison] because [buyer mechanism].
```

Evaluate **relevance, distinction, support, objection-resolution leverage, creative fit, and experimentability**. If no strong support exists, say "test hypothesis" rather than framing the claim as a research finding.

### Distinct angle examples — no invented claims

| Angle | Buyer motive | Concept direction |
|---|---|---|
| Personal style | Self-expression | "A little vintage character for everyday wear." |
| Gift consideration | Thoughtful giving | "A small gift that feels personal." |
| Product discovery | Browsing styles | "Find the ring that catches your eye." |
| Quality reassurance | Assessing finish | "Take a closer look at the details." + close-up image |
| Offer-driven | Price sensitivity | Use an offer **only if independently verified** |

## 7. Message evaluation

Adapt Wynter's messaging questions: **clarity** (what is this?), **relevance** (why this buyer?), **value** (what's in it for me?), **differentiation** (why this vs alternatives?), **brand alignment** (does it sound right?). Assess proof and remaining friction alongside these dimensions. Fix the first major breakdown before polishing phrasing.
