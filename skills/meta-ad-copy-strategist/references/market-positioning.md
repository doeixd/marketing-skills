# Market positioning: the competitive frame that makes value intelligible

**Use when:** comparing the offering to alternatives, deciding which features matter, identifying best-fit customers, or choosing a category/message angle.

## Dunford's five components — in a useful order

April Dunford begins with *real customer alternatives*, not the brand's preferred slogan:

1. **Competitive alternatives:** What would a buyer actually do if this offering did not exist? Direct competitors, substitutes, an incumbent solution, DIY, delay or buying nothing.
2. **Differentiated capabilities:** Which demonstrable attributes or capabilities matter relative to those alternatives? Do not invent a comparative advantage from an isolated feature list.
3. **Value:** So what does each capability enable or avoid for buyers? What benefit justifies choosing it?
4. **Best-fit customers:** Which buying circumstances make that value unusually important? Segmentation by motives/need is often more useful than demographics.
5. **Market category:** What familiar frame helps buyers understand the product and alternatives without excessive explanation?

Treat this as a hypothesis to test against real customers; competing candidate positions can exist, especially across separate product lines. Avoid prematurely naming or inventing a 'new category'.

## Points of parity vs points of difference

Category expectations (or points of parity) are baseline requirements to be considered a credible option. Points of difference are reasons to prefer an offering **that customers value**. A seller of elaborate rings may offer distinctive designs while still needing credible information about sizes, materials and delivery.

| Question | Example | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| What must be true just to compete? | Buyer can identify size, materials and price | Fix missing basics before emphasizing uniqueness |
| What is actually different? | Verified characteristic design style | Candidate advantage |
| Who values the difference? | Buyers wanting ornamented rather than minimalist design | Better-fit customer situation |
| Who might dislike it? | Buyers seeking understated accessories | Real preference trade-off; not every objection needs rebuttal |
| What alternative wins for them? | Plain band, vintage original, lower-priced option | Honest comparison and SKU selection |

## Avoid pseudo-differentiation

'Premium', 'high quality', 'unique', 'best-in-class', 'crafted with care' and 'designed for you' are unsupported or weak unless backed by specific proof and meaningful comparative context. Even true facts may be **category norms**, not reasons to switch. Compare **real options**, not straw-man competitors. Do not make superiority or price comparisons without current, verifiable support.

## Segmentation and targeting

Useful segmentation axes include:

- occasion (gift, replacement, event, daily use, collecting),
- sought benefit (design, price, convenience, speed, durability, status, performance),
- knowledge/consideration state (casual explorer vs category evaluator vs repeat buyer),
- constraints (budget, delivery window, fit, compatibility),
- decision role (buyer, user, recipient, influencer).

Audiences overlap; do not imply a person's motivation can be known from age or demographic status alone. **The buyer of a gift is not necessarily the user.** Prioritize segments by value fit, reachable demand, competition, evidence and acquisition feasibility.

## Positioning statement (internal, not necessarily copy)

`For [buying situation/segment], [offering] is a [recognizable category] that provides [distinct relevant value] versus [genuine alternative], because [specific credible attributes/proof].`

If any field is unknown, label it. Compare two or more candidate statements when the product fits multiple frames. A public ad should normally communicate **one** benefit, not the entire statement.

## Brand consistency vs message diversification

Positioning can remain stable while ads vary by CEP, motivation, proof, emotion, format and objection. If a headline asserts a completely different product category or promise from other brand touchpoints, investigate whether it is a new segment/position or just inconsistent messaging.
