# Brand discovery, verbal identity and copy distillation

**Use when:** an agent must *learn* a brand rather than impose a generic "friendly, premium, authentic" voice; assess how a brand describes itself versus how customers describe it; translate positioning into reusable language across channels.

Read [Workflow 3A](../workflows/03a-brand-discovery-and-messaging.md) to execute this framework, [brand discovery intake](../templates/brand-discovery-intake.md) to gather input and [messaging library template](../templates/brand-messaging-library.md) to record approved outputs.

## 1. Brand identity is evidence, not an adjective generator

Separate:
- **Intended identity:** goals, beliefs, mission, owner/founder statements, positioning ambitions.
- **Expressed identity:** repeated decisions evident in products, service, merchandising, imagery, language, pricing and packaging.
- **Perceived identity:** real consumer descriptions, expectations, recall, complaints and repeat buying behavior.

A claim such as "timeless, handcrafted luxury" can appear on the About page without being a verified production method, a customer perception or a competitively distinctive benefit. Quote as a claim of the brand until substantiated.

Map:
`brand purpose/perspective (why it exists) → product truth (what it delivers) → customer benefit (why anyone cares) → memory/recognition (how people associate it) → expression (how it speaks and looks)`.

**Brand goals are decisions, not copy promises.** "Grow gifting revenue" is an internal goal; "the perfect gift for everyone" is an unsupported outward claim.

## 2. Separate positioning, value proposition, personality, voice and tone

| Concept | Practical definition | Key evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Positioning | Relative customer-relevant value against real alternatives | Product comparison, buying situations, choice criteria |
| Value proposition | The outcome/benefit a relevant buyer can reasonably expect | Real features, use conditions and proof |
| Personality | Human traits consistently suggested by brand behavior and expression | Customer-facing decisions, creative patterns |
| Voice | Relatively stable linguistic identity across contexts | Corpus of approved copy, conversations, product descriptions |
| Tone | Situational calibration of voice for channel and circumstance | Occasion, message type, customer state |
| Distinctive assets | Recognizable cues tied to brand name and memory | Logo, product silhouette, typography, packaging, verbal patterns |
| Messaging | Reusable hierarchy of claims, promises, pillars and objection answers | Approved evidence register, buyer research |

Do not confuse eloquent prose with strong positioning, or distinctive wording with distinctiveness in the market. A brand can be recognizable but not uniquely capable; a differentiated product may be barely recognized.

## 3. Analyzing a sample corpus

Ideally examine **multiple formats and moments**: About narrative, high-performing PDPs, email, ads, FAQs and actual responses to a dissatisfied customer. Record each sample's origin, date, purpose and approval status. Treat one experimental meme or creator caption as an outlier until corroborated.

Use a practical coding sheet:

| Dimension | Observe, don't invent | Output as a writing rule |
|---|---|---|
| POV | Does the brand speak as "we", address "you", or lead with the product? | Preferred pronouns and relationship to customer |
| Lexicon | Named product terms, concrete descriptions, repeated metaphors | Preferred nouns, verbs, adjectives, forbidden generic phrases |
| Syntax | Fragments vs long periodic sentences, questions, parallel structure | Sample sentence shapes and limits |
| Rhythm | Fast, staccato, leisurely, storytelling | Typical length and variation |
| Register | Specialist, plainspoken, conversational, literary, formal | Calibrated axis with examples |
| Warmth/humor | Playful, earnest, irreverent, reserved; where is it appropriate? | Suitable and unsuitable circumstances |
| Claim posture | Precise claims, personal judgments, absolutes, caveats | How to communicate confidence and limits |
| Presentation | Emoji, capitalization, contractions, typography, CTAs | Consistency rules by channel |

**Method:** extract recurring patterns → cite exemplars → label conflicts/outliers → draft a 3–5-axis voice profile → create "sounds like us / doesn't sound like us" transformations → validate with brand owners and, when possible, customers.

Five adjectives are **not** a usable voice guide. Rules must be executable: "Use a concrete product noun in the first sentence, favor short-to-medium sentences, avoid grandiose superlatives unless supported, keep emoji out of product specifications" is more operational than "trustworthy and elevated." But these rules are examples, not defaults for every brand.

## 4. Preserve identity while shifting tone

| Setting | Tone adaptation | Identity that should persist |
|---|---|---|
| Meta Feed | Compressed, instantly relevant, concrete | Brand cue, recognizable vocabulary, truthful promise |
| Instagram Reels | Spoken, clear, natural cadence and quick visual orientation | Product terms, honest perspective, consistent humor boundary |
| PDP | Precise and reassuring; answer purchase questions | Same brand point of view, supported value proposition |
| Email | Contextual, scannable, relationship-aware | Stable syntax/lexicon and clear offer terms |
| Customer service | Calm, empathetic, accountable, direct | Trust, openness, product/policy truth |

Do not treat Instagram-specific formats as a license for forced slang, invented creator experience, unexplained POV shifts or identity confusion. A playfully irreverent public ad may need a more serious tone when addressing returns, without abandoning the underlying identity.

## 5. Customer language, persuasion and specificity

Use authentic customer speech to locate *jobs, desired outcomes, purchase criteria, fears and alternative comparisons*. It's source material for insight, not automatically public copy. Do not fabricate reviews or imply a single quote is representative.

Check every message for:
- **Desire:** what is valuable to the buyer in a real situation?
- **Difference:** what distinguishes the offer from a plausible alternative?
- **Reason to believe:** exact specification, demonstrated performance, verified policy, or authentic testimony.
- **Anxiety:** what might stop the purchase or cause future regret?
- **Association:** what should the buyer remember about the brand and when?

Turn features into benefits with `fact → consequence → valued result → supporting proof → qualifying tradeoff`. Identity or emotional meaning is a *candidate hypothesis* unless customer evidence supports it. The most compelling meaning is sometimes appearance, nostalgia or self-expression rather than physical performance.

Avoid formulaic AIDA/PAS output unless it serves a clear purpose. Specificity, relevant information and verifiability typically outperform ornamental hype as *quality criteria*; never promise a CTR or ROAS advantage without experimental evidence.

### Copy structures: optional scaffolds, chosen by situation

Classic direct-response structures are useful **sequencing aids** once the buyer situation, awareness lens and evidence are known. They are practitioner heuristics, not evidence of performance. Pick one per asset, or none.

| Structure | Sequence | Reasonable fit | Misuse to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| **AIDA** | Attention → Interest → Desire → Action | Longer sales/landing pages and cold-traffic explainers where the buyer needs a full path | Ornamental hooks; "desire" built from invented outcomes |
| **PAS** | Problem → Agitation → Solution | Short direct response, email and ads **when a real, buyer-reported problem exists** | Manufacturing or exaggerating pain for pleasure, gift or aspirational purchases; fear appeals in sensitive categories |
| **ACCA** | Awareness → Comprehension → Conviction → Action | Educational, technical or B2B offers where understanding and proof precede commitment | Skipping the comprehension step with jargon |
| **4 Cs filter** | Clear → Concise → Compelling → Credible | An **editing pass** on any draft, after structure is chosen | Treating "compelling" as permission to drop qualifiers; "credible" means traceable to a `verified` evidence ID |

Map structure to the [awareness lens](positioning-and-objections.md#3-awareness-and-message-selection): unaware audiences rarely respond to PAS agitation of a problem they don't feel; most-aware buyers usually need offer clarity, not a full AIDA arc.

**Brief the copy task explicitly (ARC-E):** state the **A**ction (exact asset and fields), the **R**ole/perspective to write from, the **C**ontext (buyer situation, awareness lens, evidence IDs, voice rules from the [marketing context file](../templates/marketing-context.md)) and the **E**valuation criteria (quality rubric, character targets, claim checks). A brief missing any of the four produces generic copy; write `[NEEDS INPUT]` for the missing part instead.

## 6. Copy compression without losing truth

Write one core proposition; then create 3–5 message pillars and map each to proof. For every copy asset preserve a claim ID and audience/angle:

`claim ledger → promise → pillar → angle/objection → copy asset → review/approval → performance learning`.

Adapt by format:
- **Brand definition:** plain, short, category first.
- **About line:** perspective, reason for existing, actual way of working.
- **Selling-point bullet:** concrete feature or experience and explicit customer payoff.
- **Headline:** one thought, preferably useful on its own; avoid repeating the primary text.
- **Description:** additional supporting detail, never a required piece of meaning on Meta.
- **Primary text:** situate buyer, convey value/proof or reduce anxiety, avoid clutter; first words must work if truncated.
- **Video overlay/script:** say/show one idea per beat, visual proof when possible, readable safe-zone text and early brand attribution.
- **CTA:** actual next step, not invented scarcity or a promise of personal transformation.

**Examples of compression (hypothetical design attributes, not manufacturer claims):**
- Fact: "Vintage-inspired patterned ring."
- Weak: "Elevate your look with our stunning, truly unique jewelry!"
- Concrete: "Vintage-inspired detailing, made to be noticed."
- Occasion angle: "For the days you want a little more character."
- Gift angle: "Looking for a ring that feels personal?"
- Product-led headline: "Explore Vintage-Inspired Rings."
The last four are *candidate concepts*, not proof of sentiment, quality, sales impact, or exclusivity.

## 7. Distinction between generic copy and ownable language

Use a **substitution test**: if a competitor could replace the brand name and every sentence would remain equally plausible, the message likely lacks a real brand-specific anchor. Fix this with more evidence, distinctive product features, point of view or actual brand cues rather than by adding superlatives.

Run an **opposition test**: would a credible competitor willingly take the opposite of the asserted position? If no ("we care about quality"), it may be a generic category expectation, not useful positioning. Don't force a provocative "enemy" to appear distinctive.

Run a **truth stress test**: would a skeptical buyer, supplier, reviewer or employee agree the claim is supported as written? If no, qualify, substantiate or remove it.

Run a **recognition test**: will customers remember the specific brand and product, not only the clever line?

## 8. Working with uncertain evidence

| Evidence situation | Agent action |
|---|---|
| No brand URL or samples | Ask for a URL or representative samples if essential; otherwise give a clearly provisional framework |
| Owner states a value, examples contradict it | Preserve both; flag tension and request a decision |
| Buyers use conflicting descriptions | Segment by buying occasion/source; don't average into meaningless adjectives |
| Customer reviews unavailable | Hypothesize possible motivations explicitly and propose research |
| Claim unsupported (handmade, sustainable, heritage, etc.) | Remove from public copy until substantiated |
| No differentiation found | Name real category parity; compete through clarity, distinctive assets, convenience, availability or improved offer as relevant |
| Voice rules unclear | Provide 2–3 plausible voice directions as proposals, not discoveries |
| Channel requires shorter text | Compress while preserving benefit, truthful qualifiers and recognizable brand cue |
