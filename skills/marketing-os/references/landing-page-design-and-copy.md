# Landing pages: structure, copy, design, forms, speed and testing (reviewed 2026-11-09)

Used by [Workflow 4D](../workflows/04d-landing-page-build.md) to build pages and by [4A](../workflows/04a-conversion-rate-optimization.md) to review them. Most published landing-page advice is practitioner experience or vendor data, not controlled research. Each section says which kind it is. Nothing here predicts a conversion lift for a particular page; it produces better hypotheses and fewer avoidable mistakes.

## 1. What a landing page is for

A landing page continues a conversation that an ad, email, search result or link started, and asks for one action. Three ideas from practitioners cover most of what goes wrong:

- **Rule of one** (Joanna Wiebe, Copyhackers): one reader, one big idea, one promise, one offer. Pages written for everyone persuade no one.
- **Conversion coupling** (Oli Gardner, Unbounce): the page must match what was clicked, in words (**message match**) and in look (**design match**). A visitor who can't see the thing they clicked for leaves.
- **Attention ratio** (Gardner): the number of things a visitor can click compared with the number of goals. A homepage may offer dozens of links; a campaign page should approach one. Gardner's supporting numbers come from Unbounce's own page data and show a modest difference, so treat this as a sound default, not a law.

## 2. Choose the page type

| Type | Use when | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Click-through page** | Selling a product or plan; next step is cart, checkout or signup | Short to medium; product, proof, price, one button |
| **Lead-capture page** | The action is a form: quote, demo, booking, download | The form and what happens next are the offer; see section 7 |
| **Long-form sales page** | Expensive, unfamiliar or high-risk purchase; cold traffic | Length follows the number of real questions, not a word target |
| **Product page as landing page** | Product-aware shoppers; catalog and shopping ads | Often the right destination for warm traffic; improve the PDP via [4B](../workflows/04b-shopify-ecommerce-cro.md) before building something new |
| **Advertorial or listicle (pre-sell page)** | Cold social traffic that needs the problem and mechanism explained before the product | Vendor claims of large gains over product pages are untested generalizations; some accounts do better sending ads straight to the product. Must be labeled as advertising (section 10) |
| **Comparison / alternatives page** | Buyers weighing you against a named option | Fair criteria, verifiable facts, say who the other option suits |
| **Pricing page** | Buyers ready to evaluate cost | Plans mapped to real segments; all fees visible ([4C](../workflows/04c-pricing-research-and-tests.md)) |
| **Waitlist / pre-launch page** | Nothing to sell yet | One promise, one field, honest date expectations |
| **Thank-you / confirmation page** | After every conversion | Confirms what happens next; see section 12 |

Awareness decides length and emphasis: the less the visitor knows, the more the page must explain before asking; a most-aware visitor mainly needs the offer and a button ([awareness lens](positioning-and-objections.md#3-awareness-and-message-selection)).

## 3. Diagnostic lenses

Use one of these to review a draft or an existing page. They are thinking tools, not formulas.

- **MECLABS conversion heuristic:** `C = 4m + 3v + 2(i − f) − 2a`: the chance of conversion depends most on the visitor's **m**otivation (which you can only match, not create), then the clarity and force of the **v**alue proposition, then **i**ncentive minus **f**riction, less **a**nxiety. MECLABS states it is not an equation to solve. Practical reading: match the traffic's intent first; make the value clear second; only then tune incentives, remove friction and answer worries.
- **LIFT model** (Chris Goward, WiderFunnel): the value proposition is lifted by **relevance**, **clarity** and **urgency**, and held down by **anxiety** and **distraction**.
- **Desire minus labour minus confusion** (Julian Shapiro, as summarized by others): increase desire by showing the outcome and proof; cut the work the visitor must do; remove anything unclear.
- **The in-person test** (Harry Dry): for each element ask, "would this help me sell if I were standing in front of the customer?" If not, remove it.

## 4. Above the fold

The best-supported fact in this area: people do scroll, but attention is concentrated at the top. In NN/g's eye-tracking analysis (120 participants, 130,000 fixations), 57% of viewing time fell above the fold and 74% within the first two screenfuls. So the first screen must let a visitor answer *what is this, is it for me, why should I care, what do I do next?*

Harry Dry's five elements are a reliable checklist:

| Element | Do | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| **Headline** | If the product is new or unusual, say plainly what it is. If it's one of many, lead with the answer to the buyer's main objection or the specific outcome. Use the buyer's own words | Slogans that would fit any company. Tests: *would it make sense with no logo above it? could a competitor say the same sentence?* |
| **Subheadline** | Say how the product delivers the headline's promise, and for whom | Repeating the headline; feature lists |
| **Visual** | Show the real product, ideally in use, at a size where details are visible | Abstract illustration and stock photos of smiling people |
| **Proof** | One short, checkable credibility cue beside the claim: rating with count and source, a recognizable customer, a number | Unattributed "trusted by thousands"; logos used without permission |
| **Call to action** | Name the value or outcome ("Get my size guide", "Start free trial"); add a few words under the button that answer the main hesitation ("No card needed", "Free returns for 30 days") | "Submit", "Learn more", several competing buttons |

Signal that more content follows (a cut-off element at the bottom of the first screen); a section that looks like the end of the page ("false floor") stops scrolling.

## 5. Below the fold

Order sections by the questions a buyer asks next. Use only what this buyer needs.

1. **Make the promise concrete:** two to four features, each written as *fact → what it does for you*, each with a visual or demonstration. The first features should prove the headline.
2. **Answer objections:** collect them from real conversations, reviews and support; answer in the customers' own words; be honest about limits and who the product is not for.
3. **Proof matched to the promise** (Dry): a promise about a feeling → customers showing it; about a change → before and after in their words; about results → numbers with context. Specific, attributed, permissioned. Never invented or cherry-picked.
4. **Offer and price:** what's included, total cost including shipping and fees, guarantee, delivery or onboarding time.
5. **FAQ:** the remaining features and objections, phrased as the questions people actually ask.
6. **Final call to action:** restate the promise with the button; there is room here to remind the visitor why.
7. **Optional founder's note:** a short, true story: the customer's situation, the problem, why you took it on, the result.

## 6. Copy

- **Source it from customers.** The strongest headlines and objection answers are lifted from reviews, interviews and support messages ([buyer research](buyer-research.md)).
- **Write plainly.** In Unbounce's 2024 benchmark of about 41,000 pages, pages written at roughly a 5th–7th grade reading level had about twice the median conversion rate of pages at a college level. This is correlational vendor data, but it agrees with long-standing usability guidance: short sentences, common words, no jargon the buyer wouldn't use.
- **Be specific.** Numbers, names, durations and conditions beat adjectives. "Ships in 2 business days" beats "fast shipping".
- **One idea per section;** headings that can be read alone as a summary of the page.
- **Microcopy matters:** button text, field labels, error messages, the line under the button, what happens after clicking.
- **Every claim traces to the claim register.** Unknowns stay `[NEEDS INPUT]`.

## 7. Forms

Evidence here is stronger than elsewhere (Baymard's large-scale usability testing):

- Ask only for what the next step needs. Every extra field is a reason to stop; qualify later where possible.
- **Labels above fields, always visible.** Placeholder text used as a label disappears when typing and causes errors.
- **Inline validation** done properly: don't flag an error before the person has finished typing, remove the message as soon as it's fixed, and still handle errors on submit.
- Use the right input types and `autocomplete` attributes so phones show the right keyboard and browsers can fill details.
- Say what happens after submitting (who contacts them, when, how) and what you will not do with their details.
- Long forms: split into steps with the easy questions first, show progress, and let people go back.
- Error messages say what went wrong and how to fix it.

## 8. Design

- **One focal action.** The primary button is the most visually prominent element on each screen; secondary actions are visibly quieter.
- **Hierarchy before decoration:** size, weight, contrast and spacing should make the reading order obvious with the styles turned down.
- **Readable type:** body text around 16px or larger on phones, line length under about 80 characters, generous line height, real contrast (WCAG AA: 4.5:1 for body text).
- **Design match:** carry the ad's imagery, colors and product shot onto the page.
- **Phone first:** most paid-social traffic is on phones. Check the first screen on a small device, keep tap targets comfortably large, and consider a persistent button on long pages. No pop-up on arrival.
- **Motion sparingly:** one purposeful moment at most; respect reduced-motion settings; nothing that delays the content.
- **Avoid the generic template look.** Anthropic's `frontend-design` skill lists the patterns that make pages look machine-made (uniform rounded cards with gradient washes, all-caps eyebrow labels over every heading, stock palettes, fade-up animation on every section) and recommends choosing a small deliberate palette and type scale for the subject. A page that looks like every other page borrows none of the brand's recognition.
- **Brand cues present:** logo, colors, typography and product presentation from the [context file](../templates/marketing-context.md).

## 9. Speed

- Aim for Google's "good" Core Web Vitals on phones at the 75th percentile: **LCP ≤ 2.5 s, INP ≤ 200 ms, CLS ≤ 0.1**. These thresholds have been stable; ignore blog posts claiming they were tightened.
- A 2020 Google/Deloitte study is widely cited as finding that a 0.1 s mobile speed improvement raised retail conversions by about 8%. It is one older study reported inconsistently; the direction (faster is better) is well supported, the exact figure is not.
- Usual causes on landing pages: an oversized hero image or video (compress, size correctly, don't lazy-load the hero), render-blocking scripts and tag managers, web fonts, third-party widgets, and images without width and height (layout shift).

## 10. Honesty, accessibility and law

- **No dark patterns:** fake countdowns or stock levels, pre-ticked boxes, hidden costs, confirm-shaming, disguised ads, back-button traps.
- **Advertorials and listicles are ads.** The FTC's native advertising guidance requires that content be identifiable as advertising: label it "Advertisement" or "Paid Advertisement" (not "Promoted" or "Presented by"), placed above or directly before the headline, in readable size and contrast, on every device. The advertiser is responsible, and claims still need substantiation. Don't imitate a news site or an independent review.
- **Accessibility:** meet WCAG 2.2 AA basics: one `h1` and headings in order, alt text, contrast, labelled fields, keyboard operation, visible focus, captions. Since June 2025 the European Accessibility Act applies to ecommerce services sold to EU consumers (in practice EN 301 549, which incorporates WCAG 2.1 AA; microenterprises providing services are exempt); US sites face ADA claims. Overlay widgets do not make a page compliant.
- **Privacy:** consent before non-essential tags where required; forms state how details are used ([privacy reference](privacy-consent-and-data-protection.md)).

## 11. Test before and after launch

Pick methods the traffic can support.

| Method | Tells you | Needs |
|---|---|---|
| **Five-second test** | Whether a stranger can say what the page offers and for whom after a glance | 5–10 people outside the project |
| **Message testing** | What target buyers find clear, relevant, believable and missing | 12–20 people who match the buyer (Peep Laja's guidance); a panel tool or recruited customers |
| **Usability test** | Where people get stuck completing the action | About 5 participants per round, on phones |
| **Session recordings and heatmaps** | Where real visitors hesitate and leave | Live traffic, consent, masking |
| **A/B test** | Which version causes more of the outcome | Enough conversions: hundreds per variant. Many pages never have this, so fix obvious problems and use the methods above instead |

Test large differences first (offer, promise, page type, proof) before button colors. Judge by the business outcome after the click (qualified leads, orders, contribution), not the page's conversion rate alone ([testing and learning](testing-and-learning.md)).

**Benchmarks:** Unbounce's 2024 report gives a median landing-page conversion rate of 6.6% across its customers' pages, with wide variation by industry. Conversion rate depends on traffic source, offer and what counts as a conversion, so compare a page with its own history, not with an industry median.

## 12. After the conversion

The confirmation page and message should: confirm exactly what was received or ordered; say what happens next and when; give one useful next step (book a time, set up, share a referral link, read the guide); and fire the conversion event once. Don't spring an unrelated upsell that makes the buyer doubt the purchase.
