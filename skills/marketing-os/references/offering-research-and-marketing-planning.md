# Offering intelligence: product/service research and go-to-market synthesis

Read with [Offering Discovery Workflow 1A](../workflows/01a-offering-discovery-and-marketing-plan.md). Use [Offering Intake](../templates/offering-discovery-intake.md) and [Offering Dossier & Marketing Plan](../templates/offering-dossier-and-marketing-plan.md) for outputs.

## A. The research model

An **offering** is more than a product specification. Separate:
1. **Core product/service:** inherent capabilities, attributes, promised experience, scope and limits.
2. **Commercial offer:** configuration, price, terms, packaging, onboarding, fulfillment, bundles, financing or subscriptions.
3. **Buying experience:** brand trust, information, comparison, demonstration, accessibility, checkout/sales process, support.
4. **Buyer context:** jobs, problems, desires, occasions, alternatives, decision participants and risk.
5. **Business constraints:** delivery and unit economics, capacity, stock, compliance and operational readiness.

Product owners tend to overvalue features they built; buyers may seek speed, beauty, lower perceived risk, a memorable gift, a more confident decision or an experience. Some offerings create *pleasure* without eliminating any pain. Never force problem-solution language onto an aesthetic, collectible or identity product.

### Brand relationship

Start with existing approved brand profile where possible: identity, personality, voice, positioning, distinctive assets, advantages and nonnegotiable claims. Run [Brand Discovery 3A](../workflows/03a-brand-discovery-and-messaging.md) when absent and material. A preliminary brand scan is enough to start factual product research; don't wait for a full brand archetype exercise. Compare product-specific promises with brand-wide promises and flag contradictions. One brand may contain offerings with different best-fit buyers and separate value propositions.

## B. Map jobs, pains and gains with a value map

[Christensen Institute's JTBD](https://www.christenseninstitute.org/theory/jobs-to-be-done/) centers *progress in circumstances*, including functional, emotional and social dimensions. [Strategyzer's Value Proposition Canvas](https://www.strategyzer.com/library/the-value-proposition-canvas) maps customer **jobs, pains and gains** against offering **products/services, pain relievers and gain creators**.

For *each distinct buying situation*, ask:
- What triggered consideration? Why this moment?
- Who buys, uses, pays, recommends and approves?
- What outcome defines success? What does buyer value, avoid, signal or feel?
- Which alternative is current, and why is it good enough?
- What forces encourage switching? What anxieties or habits keep the status quo?
- Which pains matter most by severity/frequency, which gains by value?
- What proof makes the offering plausible to that decision-maker?
- What happens after purchase: adoption effort, satisfaction, repeat needs, returns?

**Evidence:** customer interviews, observed choice behavior, actual reviews/returns, support, onsite search and category data. Descriptions of buyer motives from an AI without those sources remain **hypotheses**. Separate buyer, user, recipient and payer especially for gifts, enterprise and healthcare/professional services. Avoid assuming sensitive personal attributes in ad copy.

## C. Position relative to alternatives, not the product backlog

[April Dunford](https://www.aprildunford.com/post/a-quickstart-guide-to-positioning) advocates **competitive alternatives → differentiated features/capabilities → value → best-fit customers → category**. The competitor list must represent what *real buyers consider*, which may include doing nothing. An advantage counts only when it matters to someone, and market category choices change expectations about capabilities and price.

| Dimension | Investigate | Counter-question |
|---|---|---|
| Parity | Which basic category expectations must be met? | Is this merely table stakes? |
| Advantage | What observable difference exists? | Is it documented compared with a real alternative? |
| Value | What benefit does the difference create? | Does the buyer care and can they articulate why? |
| Fit | Which situation prioritizes that benefit? | Who would value a different tradeoff instead? |
| Proof | What supports the claimed outcome? | Is evidence representative and current? |
| Availability | Can the buyer find/get it in the needed channel/time? | Would access defeat preference? |

Reject unsupported "best," "only," "guaranteed," "premium" and "handcrafted" language. A competitor's ad is evidence of its claimed positioning, not of sales effectiveness.

## D. From feature to buyer meaning, both positive and negative

Use a **dual-sided feature ledger**:

`feature → mechanical/experiential consequence → functional gain or desire → emotional/social interpretation → proof → buyer circumstance`

and

`feature → constraint/tradeoff → which buyer is disadvantaged → mitigation/qualification`.

Example: ornate vintage styling offers distinct appearance to someone drawn to ornamental jewelry but may be unsuitable for someone seeking minimalism. Avoid representing this as demonstrated customer research until there is customer evidence.

Decide the copy tier:
- **Feature/specification copy**: for comparison/validation and precise shopping.
- **Benefit copy**: explains a verifiable consequence of a capability.
- **Situation/job copy**: connects product with the triggering occasion.
- **Emotional/identity copy**: interprets aesthetic or social meaning without claiming what all customers feel.
- **Proof/reassurance copy**: supports a purchase decision with genuine detail/policies.

Do not fill every ad with every tier: choose the single strongest point for the given buyer stage and medium.

## E. Decision objections, weaknesses, and risk reduction

Classify obstacles:

| Classification | Example | Proper intervention |
|---|---|---|
| Knowledge gap | "What are the measurements?" | Provide accurate, visible specifications |
| Credibility gap | "Does it work/last?" | Real proof, trial, validated reviews, warranted outcomes |
| Fit mismatch | "Not my taste/needs" | Different buyer/occasion; avoid pretending universal fit |
| Actual disadvantage | Higher effort, fewer variants, limited delivery | Fix product/process, qualify or position for buyers who accept tradeoff |
| Financial concern | Total cost vs substitutes | Explain value with evidence, improve price/terms only if viable |
| Process friction | Long forms, unclear checkout, opaque service scope | UX/service operations fix |
| Risk and regret | "What if it doesn't fit?" | Actual returns/sizing/help; revise policy if needed |
| Timing | Needs it after/before certain date | Verify actual delivery or onboarding availability |

An assurance must be **real**: refunds, warranties, certification, reviews, support and quality control cannot be invented by copy generation. An honest drawback can focus positioning and reduce irrelevant inquiries. Don't assume that better persuasion can fix poor delivery, unsafe products or bad economics.

Baymard's [D2C research](https://baymard.com/research/direct-to-consumer) highlights how product detail, brand evidence, reviews and path design support buyers in brand-owned stores. For physical products, practical content often includes scale, variants, dimensions, materials, use context, delivery and returns.

## F. Service and intangible-offer extensions

The [7Ps](https://www.aqa.org.uk/resources/business/as-and-a-level/business-7131-7132/teach/teaching-guide-7ps-of-the-marketing-mix) include **Product, Price, Place, Promotion, People, Process and Physical Evidence**. For services, emphasize:
- **Service scope**: included tasks, deliverables, exclusions, revision limits and who does the work.
- **Delivery system**: steps, timeline, milestones, handoffs, implementation and client input.
- **Credibility**: authentic examples, credentials where verified, demonstrations, case studies with qualifying context.
- **Outcome control**: which results are within the provider's control versus client or market dependence.
- **Capacity**: availability, booking/lead time, service quality variance, ongoing support.
- **Commercial reality**: fixed vs hourly vs retainer pricing, payment schedule, cancellation, implementation cost and time to value.
- **Decision participants**: champion, budget holder, procurement, legal/security, end user.

For digital/SaaS, add setup, platform compatibility, integration limits, security/privacy statements only if documented, reliability, contract renewals, data portability and adoption. Service evidence should be more than self-asserted expertise. See the [Open University/CIM marketing mix summary](https://www.open.edu/openlearn/mod/oucontent/olink.php?id=104179&targetdoc=7Ps+A+brief+summary+of+marketing+and+how+it+works) on uncertainty and physical evidence.

## G. Pricing and profitability

Price is both a customer sacrifice and sometimes a quality/positioning cue, but **price is not proof of quality**. Compare **total cost to customer**, offer contents and conditions rather than cherry-picked sticker prices. Distinguish:
- Price-tier and alternative anchoring (actual prices, clearly dated);
- Perceived fairness and buyer priorities;
- Gross vs contribution margin after shipping, platform fees, discounts, returns, packaging and fulfillment;
- Customer acquisition and payback constraints;
- For services: delivery hours, subcontractor costs, capacity, lifetime obligations;
- For subscriptions: renewal, cancellation, retention, onboarding and payment uncertainty.

[Strategyzer's testing sequence](https://www.strategyzer.com/library/roadmap-to-test-your-value-proposition) separates validating **customer jobs**, **valued features**, and **willingness to pay**. Interest and self-reported intention are weaker than actual purchases; experimentation must not mislead users. Don't invent a "20% off" or "limited spots" offer. If price cannot be evaluated because costs are missing, give scenario formulas and flag missing inputs.

## H. Sources, claims and legal checks

Maintain a ledger `claim_id, exact statement, origin URL/doc, date, evidence status, qualification, license/permission, reviewer, copy uses`.

Suggested evidence order (context-dependent): verified product/contract/policy and direct measurement; independently verified testing; authentic, contextual customer reports; seller statements; indirect inference; hypothesis; unknown. A product page can substantiate that the **seller states** a feature, not necessarily that the feature performs as claimed.

For US advertising, the [FTC small-business advertising FAQ](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/advertising-faqs-guide-small-business) describes standards for truthful, substantiated claims and honest testimonials; obtain specialist legal review where regulated. Check current [Meta Advertising Standards](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/), privacy permissions and creative rights before publication. Avoid false urgency, deceptive comparison and personal-attribute assertions. Don't cite awards or guarantees without verifying applicability and terms.

## I. Distilling into marketing assets and tests

Use [dossier template](../templates/offering-dossier-and-marketing-plan.md):
1. **Plain definition** — what is it, what does buyer receive?
2. **Buyer situations** — why now, what they want, what they'd do without it?
3. **Key benefits** — up to three prioritized verified value pillars and honest limits.
4. **Alternatives/positioning** — why this for this buyer, where another option wins.
5. **Offer/assurances** — price and conditions, genuine quality signals, actual policies.
6. **Copy system** — evidence-tagged feature→benefit bullets, short and long descriptions, headlines, Meta ad units, FAQ, sales talking points and visual hooks.
7. **Creative** — demonstrations or photographic proof, format/placement/brand cues.
8. **Marketing plan** — market readiness, channel-by-intent choice, PDP/offer fixes, campaign hypotheses, budget/profit guardrails, review and learning.

Don't assume every product should run Meta ads. Existing search intent, affiliates, marketplaces, lifecycle CRM, organic/community content, partnerships or sales outreach may be more efficient depending on where customers already look and what buying proof they need. Meta Blueprint's [creative diversification lesson](https://www.facebookblueprint.com/student/activity/707402) supports genuinely varied formats, visuals and messages; volume of superficial paraphrases is not a strategy.

**Measurement:** define purchase, contribution, qualified lead or repeat outcomes before launch. CTR and reach are diagnostics, not proof of profitability. Apply incrementality tests where feasible; don't overclaim causes from platform attribution alone.

## J. Research vs recommendations

The final dossier must mark:
- **Known** — substantiated product facts, customer behavior or business metrics.
- **Reported** — buyer/vendor claims with source and limits.
- **Hypothesized** — plausible jobs, objections or language needing validation.
- **Recommended** — the marketer's proposed interventions, ranking and test priority.
- **Blocked** — actions requiring missing policy, legal, margin or delivery confirmation.

A high-quality product investigation can conclude **"improve the product/service or page before advertising"**. That is a valid strategic success, not a failure to generate copy.
