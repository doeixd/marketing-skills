# Meta advertising policy and substantiation review

This is a **risk-screening checklist**, not legal advice or a prediction of Meta approval. Always consult [Meta Advertising Standards](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/) and the account's current policy status; policy and enforcement can change.

## 1. Claim substantiation

Every claim capable of influencing a buying decision needs a traceable source. Common pitfalls: "handmade", "hypoallergenic", "American-made", "100% genuine", "clinically proven", "best-selling", "#1", "lifetime quality", "risk free", "free shipping", "60% off", "arrives tomorrow", "limited stock". None should be generated from aesthetic cues, likely materials or category stereotypes.

- **Features / specs:** official product records + variant match.
- **Pricing / promotion:** current price, dates, eligibility, exclusions, tax/shipping context.
- **Reviews / social proof:** authentic, permissioned, appropriately represented, no invented counts or endorsements.
- **Comparative claims:** comparable basis, current evidence, context, no fabricated competitor faults.
- **Scarcity / urgency:** actual deadline or inventory constraints, not pressure fabricated for persuasion.
- **Outcomes / before-and-after:** appropriate substantiation and current category rules. Treat regulated or sensitive categories as high risk.

An unverified adjective may still create an objective impression; e.g., "premium quality" deserves scrutiny when coupled with claims about durability or materials. If unsure, ask for evidence or state a limited, observable description.

## 2. Personal attributes and sensitive assumptions

Meta disallows ads that assert or imply sensitive personal attributes. Do not write copy that directly presumes the viewer has a particular medical condition, race, religion, age, sexual orientation, financial condition, or other protected/sensitive status. Safer patterns describe the product, a general situation, or an offer rather than diagnosing the reader.

- Risky: "Are you drowning in debt?" → safer: "Explore tools for understanding repayment options" (subject to applicable financial services rules).
- Risky: "Suffering from severe anxiety?" → safer: "Learn about our stress-management resources" (subject to health rules).
- For ordinary jewelry ads, focus on style, design, occasions and verified product traits instead of assigning personal attributes.

Do not infer sensitive traits or exploit vulnerability to choose angles. Some product categories also carry restrictions beyond copywording; review the category-specific Meta rules.

## 3. Landing page and experience alignment

Compare ad, media, landing page, cart and policies for contradictions. Check direct product availability, country eligibility, price, variant, delivery estimate, returns, subscriptions, fees and claims. Disclaimers should not secretly reverse the headline. A misleading destination cannot be fixed by deleting a few adjectives from the ad.

## 4. Real drawback handling

Acknowledge product limitations in analysis. If a concern cannot be answered, mark `unresolved` and recommend action rather than invent reassurance. When a limitation is material to the purchase, make information accessible at the point of decision. Examples: no half sizes; plated finish requires care; preorder lead time; personalization is nonreturnable — **only if verified for the actual product**.

## 5. Risk categories, manual review

Escalate financial products/services, health claims, supplements, weight loss, political ads, housing/employment, and other restricted/special categories. This skill doesn't replace a domain-specific compliance assessment or legal review. For every ad:

```text
claim -> exact evidence -> status -> affected placement/variant -> decision (approve draft | rewrite | block pending proof)
```

The agent must avoid promising that its output "will be approved". If a policy is inaccessible or unclear, mention the limitation.

## 6. Red-team pass

Read the ad as a skeptical customer and as a policy reviewer:

1. Does it imply knowledge about the reader's sensitive traits?
2. Does a claim sound measurable or comparative but lack evidence?
3. Could a buyer reasonably interpret it more strongly than the evidence permits?
4. Are discounts, scarcity, guarantees and delivery assertions real and current?
5. Are the image, description, CTA and landing page consistent?
6. Does a dynamic ad apply a true statement to every represented SKU?
7. Is there an objection concealed by wording that should instead be addressed by product/policy?
